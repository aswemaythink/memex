# The story

> Written 2026-10-13, the day memex found its story. Draft copy for the
> site's landing page, and the reason this project exists.

## 1945

Vannevar Bush ran American science through the Second World War. When it
ended, what kept him up at night wasn't weapons. It was the record. Science
was producing findings faster than anyone could find them again. Good work
was being lost, not because it was wrong, but because nobody could get back
to it.

In July 1945 he published "As We May Think" in *The Atlantic*. In it he
imagined a desk: two slanted translucent screens, a keyboard, levers, and
inside, on microfilm, every book, letter and note you owned. He called it
the **memex**.

Storage wasn't the point. The point was **trails**. Libraries index by
alphabet and category; minds work by association. On the memex you would
put two documents side by side, press a button, and tie them together. Do
it again and again, add your own notes, and you've laid a trail through the
material: your reasoning, kept, so you or anyone you handed it to could walk
it later.

Bush predicted a new profession: **trail blazers**, people who take delight
in making useful paths through the common record.

The memex was never built. But Douglas Engelbart read the essay as a young
radar technician in 1945 and spent his life on it, which became the 1968
demo that gave us the mouse and hypertext. Ted Nelson named hypertext with
Bush in mind. From there, the web. Every link you've ever clicked descends
from that desk.

## The camera and the trail

The same essay also imagines a walnut-sized camera strapped to a
scientist's forehead, photographing everything he sees. Capture everything
and sort it out later.

Two ideas, one essay. The camera is the one we keep rebuilding. The trail is
the one that changed the world: associations made **on purpose**, by
someone who understood the material.

## Now

Eighty years later, the flood is back, and this time agents produce it.
Every run writes logs, reports, notes and handoffs into folders nobody will
open again. The answer the industry reached for is the camera: hooks that
record everything, servers, vector stores, LLMs summarising the pile after
the fact.

memex takes Bush's other idea.

## memex

memex is **authored context compression**: trails of **intent**, for humans
and agents.

Code records what a system does. The intent layer records what it's *for*:
a README says why a folder exists, a header says what a file is for, a case
says what's claimed and how it's proven, and a log says what an agent was
trying and what it found. Whoever understood it best compresses it, at the
moment they understood it. Citations become trails, and memex walks them
both ways.

Developers and agents are the trail blazers. An agent that finishes a job
compresses what it learned and cites what it touched; the next agent walks
that trail instead of starting from zero. Logs are the camera's film: kept while
the work is live, then thrown away. What's still true gets authored into the
layer.

No server, no database, no model in the loop. Markdown files and a CLI.
Because the compression was already done, by whoever understood it.

## Why this matters to us

When the story came out, the reaction was goosebumps. It wasn't the
history alone. It was recognising that the thing we'd been designing for
practical reasons (READMEs everywhere, headers, case ids cited in prose,
logs that expire, no automated capture) was Bush's idea all along, and
that it had been the right idea for eighty years. The camera has always
been tempting. The trail has always been what mattered.

That feeling is the bar for the site, the README and the tool: it should
make people feel what we felt.
